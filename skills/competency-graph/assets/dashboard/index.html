General definitions

Every rule and term behind the numbers on this page, each explained once. Jump to a topic:

Competencies and levels

Personal competency and framework competency

The page compares two kinds of competency. Keeping them apart explains most of its columns.

KindWhat it isWhose it isIts level
Personal competencySomething the owner of this graph knows or can do, kept as a note with the evidence behind it.The owner’s. No two people have the same set.The level the owner claims, and the level the evidence supports.
Framework competencyA requirement that a framework defines, with a description and what L1 to L4 mean for it.The framework’s: a professional body’s, or one the owner wrote. It is the same for everyone who uses the framework.The level a pathway or a job spec requires.

A personal competency is mapped to each framework competency it answers to. One personal competency can answer to several frameworks. Where several personal competencies are mapped to one framework competency, the highest level among them counts.

Levels: L1 Aware to L4 Expert

Every competency, personal or framework, is rated on the same four levels.

LevelWhat it meansComparable to
L1 AwareUnderstands it and can discuss it, but has not delivered it.RICS APC level 1 (knowledge); Dreyfus novice and advanced beginner.
L2 PractitionerDelivers it independently in normal situations.RICS APC level 2 (application); Dreyfus competent.
L3 AdvancedHandles complex cases without guidance, is accountable for the result and guides others.RICS APC level 3 (reasoned advice); Dreyfus proficient; SFIA level 5.
L4 ExpertA recognised authority beyond their own team: others rely on their judgement.The FRICS “expert” characteristic; the top of the US OPM and NIH scales (“recognized authority”); SFIA levels 6 and 7.

A competency with no claimed level shows as Not rated. A framework’s own scale (RICS levels, Six Sigma belts, CFA exam levels) is restated on L1 to L4 before it is used here.

Claimed, assessed and supported

A personal competency can carry up to three levels. None of them replaces another, and the differences between them are what the page is for.

ReadingWho sets itThe question it answers
ClaimedThe owner, in the note or in a Claimed dropdown.What do I say I can do?
AssessedOne or more assessors (a supervisor, counsellor, manager or peer), each rating recorded with their name and the date.What does someone who has seen my work say?
SupportedNo one: it is worked out from the evidence, by the rule in the next section.What could a stranger verify from the record?

The Assessed column stays out of view until an assessor has rated something. An assessor rates against the wording of each level, not against the evidence counts, so an assessed level can sit above the supported one.

Several assessors can rate the same competency, and each one’s rating is kept. The assessed level is then the lowest of them, the level every assessor supports, and it is the one a job spec is scored on. Where they differ, the Assessed level carries a mark, 2 for two levels apart, and Rating gaps lists each assessor’s rating. A difference is not an error: it shows where the people who have seen the work do not yet agree.

How the supported level is worked out

The supported level is the highest level whose requirements the evidence meets. It is computed, never typed in, and it changes only when the evidence does.

What each level needs

Supported levelWhat the evidence must show
L1 AwareAny evidence at all. A course or a credential on its own supports L1 and no higher, and so does delivered work that adds up to less than L2 asks for.
L2 PractitionerDelivered work adding up to six months or more. The work need not have been led.
L3 AdvancedTwo or more units of led work, at two or more organisations or with an anchor, adding up to five or more years led.
L4 ExpertEverything L3 needs, with ten or more years led and at least one kind of outside recognition.

L2 asks for time as well as a piece of work, because a single day’s work or the first weeks of a new job show exposure, not independent delivery. Delivered work with no dates cannot be counted, so it supports L1 as well. The Missing for the next level column says how many months are still needed.

The top two levels rest on led work and on time, not on the number of things touched: years of experience alone predict performance poorly, and an expert is defined by what others rely on.

Delivering the work and leading it

The rule separates two things a person can have done with a competency. Delivering it earns L2. Leading it is what L3 and L4 ask for, and no amount of delivered work that was never led reaches them.

QuestionDelivering the workLeading the work
What it meansThe owner did the work, or part of it, to a result. Someone else may have set the direction and answered for the outcome.The owner took the lead on this competency in that work: made the call, gave the reasoned advice or owned the result.
What it is notReading about it, watching it done or passing a course in it.Managing people. Leading here is about the work, so a sole practitioner can lead and a manager may not have.
Which evidence countsAny unit except a course or a credential: a role, an engagement, an activity, a project or a publication.Roles, engagements and activities. A project, publication or course counts only when it carries outside recognition, because the owner’s own projects show practice, not standing.
How it is recordedThe evidence lists the competency among those it shows.The evidence marks the competency as led, one competency at a time. A role can be led for one competency and only delivered for another.
What it supportsL2 Practitioner, once it adds up to six months.L3 Advanced and L4 Expert. Led work is also delivered work, so it counts towards L2 as well.
An examplePreparing the rent comparables for a renewal that a manager then priced.Setting the renewal rent, advising the owner to accept it and answering for the result.

Kinds of outside recognition

L4 Expert needs at least one kind, on any evidence of the competency or of a narrower one beneath it.

KindWhat counts

AssessingAssessing or judging others’ work: an exam assessor, a peer reviewer, an award juror.

Broader and narrower competencies

Evidence for a competency also supports every broader competency above it, up to its domain. So work on a specialty counts for its discipline too.

There is one limit. A broader competency reaches L3 or L4 only from led work recorded on it directly, or when at least one narrower competency beneath it reaches that level on its own. Many narrow competencies each led once do not add up to an expert.

Years of work and years led

Years of work is how long the owner has done work that shows the competency. Years led is the part of that time in which the owner was leading it. The first is about exposure and the second about accountability, so years led is normally the smaller figure and the one the higher levels are tested on.

MeasureWhich work countsWhose datesWhat it is used for
Months of delivered workAll delivered work that shows the competency, whether the owner led it or not.The unit’s. A project inside a role counts the role’s whole period; a project on its own counts its own dates.The L2 test: six months or more.
Years of workThe same idea over a career: the roles, engagements and activities that show the competency, led or not. Where there is no such work, projects and courses are used.The unit’s. A role’s whole period counts for every competency it shows, so the figure is an upper limit.Context only. It shows how long the owner has worked with the competency. No level is tested on it.
Years ledOnly the work marked as led for this competency.Each led note’s own. A project led inside a long role counts the project’s period, not the role’s. A led project with no date of its own takes its role’s period.The L3 test (five years) and the L4 test (ten years).

An example: nine years in a role that shows Leasing, with one two-year project inside it marked as led for Leasing, gives nine years of work and two years led. If the role itself were marked as led for Leasing, both figures would be nine.

A date recorded only to the month or the year counts from the first day for a start and to the last day for an end. Work still under way runs to the day the graph was read.

Deemed claims

A deemed claim is a level the owner attests without documented evidence. The usual case is a professional body’s mandatory competency held through membership. It shows as Attested (deemed). It is never counted as evidence, so it does not change the supported level.

Comparing a claim with the evidence

Fit and the ▲ ▼ markers

Fit compares the claimed level with the supported level. The claimed level is the one the Claimed dropdown shows, so an unsaved or saved change counts at once.

FitMarkerWhat it means
MatchesNoneThe claim equals what the evidence supports.
Rated above the evidence▲The claim is higher than the evidence supports. Add evidence, or lower the claim. ▲1 beside a Supported level means one level above.
Rated below the evidence▼The evidence supports more than is claimed, so the claim could be raised. ▼2 means two levels below.
Not ratedNoneNo level is claimed. Where there is evidence, the Use LX button takes the supported level.
No evidenceA dashed outlineNothing that can be shared supports the competency yet, whether it is rated or not.

Stale evidence

A competency is stale when its latest eligible evidence ended more than — years before the day the graph was read. Which evidence is eligible depends on the kind of competency:

Kind of competencyEvidence that keeps it current
A practiceRoles, engagements and activities. A side project does not reset the clock on a practice that has work behind it. A practice shown only by projects or courses falls back to those.
A method, tool or body of knowledgeRoles, engagements, activities, projects and courses.

A credential supports a claim but never keeps it current. Only a competency marked active is flagged. Staleness is reported only: it never lowers a level. Someone who was an expert and stopped practising is an expert whose evidence is stale. On the timeline, units that ended before the stale line are hatched.

Prerequisites

A competency can name others it requires. The rule is about the claims: a competency should not be claimed above the level claimed for one it requires, and one that is not rated counts as below every level. Rating gaps lists each claim that stands above a prerequisite. It is a report on the claims being consistent with each other. It never changes a claim, and it plays no part in the supported level, which comes from the evidence alone.

Framework pathways

On Competency frameworks, each requirement of a pathway is compared with the personal competency mapped to it.

Evidence: Meets, Short or None

Evidence compares the supported level with the level the pathway requires.

EvidenceWhat it means
MeetsThe supported level reaches the required level.
ShortThere is some evidence, but the supported level is below the required level.
NoneNo evidence that can be shared supports the requirement.

Short measures against the requirement, not against the claim. A claim that matches its evidence exactly is still Short when the pathway asks for a higher level: claimed L2 and supported L2 against a required L3, for example.

Basis

Basis says what the requirement rests on today.

BasisWhat it means
EvidencedDocumented evidence reaches the required level.
Attested (deemed)The owner attests the level without documented evidence. It is never counted as evidence.
Claimed onlyThe claim reaches the required level, and the evidence does not yet.
Below LXNeither the claim nor the evidence reaches the required level, LX.

A competency the candidate chooses needs L2.

Group minimums: Mandatory, Core and Optional

Some pathways group their competencies (RICS APC, Chan AFSE, Chan AGEN, Chan CXMDS).

GroupWhat it holds
MandatoryCompetencies required of every pathway in the same field, at the same levels.
CoreThe baseline of this one pathway.
OptionalA list the candidate chooses from, under the group’s rule (“three to Level 3”, for example).

Each group’s heading row says whether its minimum is met, judged two ways:

FlagJudged on
As claimedThe levels the Claimed dropdowns show, deemed claims included.
As evidencedSupported levels only.
  • A minimum is met when every fixed level in the group is met and its selection rule can be satisfied.
  • Where a guide asks for one of several competencies at a level (“A or B”), one of them at that level is enough.
  • Where at most one of several choices may count, only the highest of them is counted.
  • A choice that must be taken to a set level counts only at that level or above. Below it, it is treated as not chosen.
  • Where a group lists its competencies in sub-groups (the specialisations within the Optional lists of Chan AFSE, Chan AGEN and Chan CXMDS), the rule and the minimum cover the whole group. The sub-groups only say where each competency belongs.
  • When two personal competencies map to one framework competency, each gets a row, and the group minimum counts only the better one.

Job spec scores

A job spec lists framework competencies in three sections, Mandatory, Core and Optional, each with a required level. The level held for each is the highest among the personal competencies mapped to it, read as claimed or as supported (and as assessed, once an assessor has rated).

Result of each requirement

ResultWhat it means
MetThe level held equals the level required.
AboveThe level held is higher than the level required.
ShortA level is held, but it is below the level required.
Not heldNo level is held: nothing is mapped to the requirement, or what is mapped has no level on the basis chosen.
HeldIn Optional only: a level is held. Optional requirements are never short.

Short measures the level held against the level required. It does not compare the claim with the evidence, so a claim that matches its evidence can still be Short.

Breadth, depth and credit above the requirement

Each Mandatory and Core requirement earns points for the level held, up to the level required.

LevelBreadth pointsDepth points
L1 Aware11
L2 Practitioner23
L3 Advanced39
L4 Expert427
ScoreWhat it shows
BreadthHow much of the spec is covered: breadth points earned as a share of the breadth points required.
DepthThe same share in depth points, which weigh the higher requirements most: one L3 counts as three L2s.
Credit above the requirementDepth points for levels held above what is required, with everything held in Optional. It is counted apart and never fills a gap elsewhere.

On the scorecard these are three columns, headed with the levels being scored: Claimed breadth, Claimed depth and Claimed credit above when it scores your claimed levels, and Supported in place of Claimed when it scores the evidence. Breadth and depth stop at the level required. So L4 held against an L3 requirement shows 9 / 9 in depth, and the other 18 points (27 less 9) show as +18 in the credit column.

For example, a requirement at L3 that is held at L2 earns 2 of 3 breadth points and 3 of 9 depth points. Percentages are rounded down.

The mandatory gate and the bands

The mandatory gate comes first. After it, the band goes by the lower of breadth % and depth % on Mandatory and Core.

BandWhen
Mandatory not metA Mandatory competency is below its level, whatever the totals say.
Below probationThe lower score is under the spec’s probation floor: 80% unless the spec sets another.
ProbationThe lower score is from the probation floor up to 99%.
BaselineBoth scores are 100%: every Mandatory and Core requirement is met.
Exceeds roleBaseline, with credit above the requirement worth at least the spec’s share of its depth target: 20% unless the spec sets another.

Promotion

Promotion is shown when the spec names the next role up. It reads Ready when both of these hold:

  • this spec is at Baseline or better;
  • the next role’s mandatory gate is met, and the lower of its two scores is at or above its own probation floor.

The kind of gap

DiagnosisWhenWhat it tells you
Broad but shallowDepth % is more than one point below breadth %.The shortfalls are where the higher levels are required.
Deep but narrowBreadth % is more than one point below depth %.Lower-level requirements are missing.
Short evenlyThe two are within one point of each other.The shortfalls are spread across the requirements.

What is counted and what is left out

Disclosure

Every piece of evidence carries one of three disclosure levels, and the page is built for one audience.

DisclosureWho may see itOn this page
PublicAnyone. It can appear anywhere.Counted.
On requestA named reader who asks, such as an employer reading an application.Counted. The figures are what a reader given on-request evidence could be shown.
ConfidentialNo one outside the owner’s files.Left out of every level, count, year and chart, along with any unit whose job is confidential.

So a supported level here can be lower than the owner’s full record would give. “No evidence” means nothing that can be shared, not nothing at all.

Industry sectors

A sector is the business where the work was done; for an engagement, that is the client’s sector. Own projects, courses and credentials have none: that is no gap in the record, and the heatmap counts them in a column of their own, Own work & credentials. It shades the sectors against each other and leaves that column out of the scale, since it would otherwise set the maximum.

How this works
What the page and the skill behind it are: how the graph is built, stored and counted, and how a change made here reaches the record.

Change a claimed level

  1. Find the competency on Rate personal competencies (type in Search, or use the Domain, Fit and Framework filters), or anywhere else a Claimed dropdown appears.
  2. Pick the new level in its Claimed dropdown, or press Use LX to take the level the evidence supports. The dropdown is outlined and the row says unsaved; the ▲ and ▼ markers update at once.
  3. Press Save changes in the bar at the top. The outline turns dashed and the row says saved. Discard drops changes that aren't saved yet.
  4. Ask Claude to apply my dashboard changes. Claude writes the saved levels into the notes, which stay the record, and refreshes the page. Until then, a saved change is listed above the Rate personal competencies table, where Withdraw takes it back.

Saving needs edit or contributor access to this dashboard. Anyone else can read the page; to change a level, they ask the owner or Claude.

Have assessors rate the competencies

A supervisor, counsellor, manager or peer can rate the same competencies, and more than one of them can. Each assessor’s rating is kept beside the owner’s own in an Assessed column and never replaces it, or another assessor’s.

  1. The ownerLists the assessorsOn Configuration, Add an assessor: a name and, if it helps, a role.
  2. An assessorPicks their nameUnder Rate as assessor, above a table with an Assessed column.
  3. An assessorRatesPicks a level in each Assessed dropdown, seeing only their own ratings.
  4. An assessorSavesSave changes keeps each rating under their name, with the day’s date.
  5. ClaudeAppliesAsked to apply the dashboard changes, writes the ratings into the notes.
  6. The ownerComparesClaimed against assessed, and one assessor against another on Rating gaps.

Keep the list of assessors

  • On Configuration, under Assessors, press Add an assessor, type the name and, if it helps, the role (supervisor, counsellor, manager, peer), and press Save assessor. It is saved to this dashboard at once.
  • Edit changes a name or a role. When Claude applies the change, ratings recorded under the old name move to the new one.
  • Delete asks first. The ratings that person gave stay in the notes under their name; they can no longer be picked.
  • Use the list in your settings file drops the changes made here and goes back to the list Claude last applied.

Give the ratings

  1. Above the Rate personal competencies table, a pathway or a job spec’s scorecard, pick the assessor under Rate as assessor. The Assessed column turns into dropdowns, each showing that assessor’s own rating and nothing of anyone else’s, so that each assessor rates without seeing the others.
  2. The assessor reads each level’s wording on Framework competency definitions and picks the level in the Assessed dropdown. Not rated takes their rating away.
  3. Press Save changes. Each rating is saved under the assessor picked when it was made, with the day’s date, so a second assessor can pick their own name and carry on before anything is saved.
  4. Pick No one to finish. The column stays in view, as plain levels, once any rating is recorded.
  5. Ask Claude to apply my dashboard changes. The ratings go into the notes and the assessors into the settings file. A new rating replaces only that assessor’s last one.

Read the result

  • The Assessed level is the lowest of the assessors’ ratings: the level every one of them supports. With one assessor it is simply theirs. Hover over it to see who gave what, and when.
  • Where assessors differ, the level carries a mark such as 2, for two levels apart, and Rating gaps lists the competency with each assessor’s rating, widest difference first.
  • A difference is not an error and nothing needs correcting. It shows where to talk; an assessor who changes their mind picks their name and rates again.
  • On Job specs, Score on: Assessed levels scores a role on the assessed level, beside the scores as claimed and as supported.

Record new work

The levels on this page rest on written records of what was done: one file for each job or engagement, which the graph points at. A record kept while the work is fresh is the difference between a claim that can be backed and one that cannot. This page does not edit a record; Claude does, with you.

  3. Answer one batch of questions on the gaps: a missing number or its baseline, your own part in shared work, what you led.
  4. Claude writes each line to the standard below, reads it again against its source, and adds it to the record. Work not yet delivered is kept apart until it ships.
  5. Claude proposes the links into the graph: the competencies the work shows, the ones you led, any outside recognition. Approve them, then ask Claude to refresh my dashboard.

What a strong line holds

A line that will stand up in a CV or an interview has four parts. The standard is called HAM-Z, after them.

Cut average lease cycle time by 50% by leading the company-wide roll-out of the VTS leasing platform, moving the whole deal pipeline online from offer to signing.

PartWhat it isIn the example
H: hard skill or toolA named tool, system, method, standard or statute that a reader could look up.The VTS leasing platform.
A: actionWhat you did, and how much of it was yours: built, cut, led, negotiated.Cut; leading the roll-out.
M: metricA number from a source, with its baseline and period where they exist.Lease cycle time, by 50%.
Z: processHow the result came about, specific enough that it could not describe someone who did it another way.A company-wide roll-out that moved the deal pipeline online.

The number is the gate. A line with no number from a source is kept as a supporting point, and no number is ever estimated to get it through. It becomes a full line the day its number turns up.

How this works

This page is the visible end of a larger system. The record behind it is a competency graph: a folder of plain text notes in the owner’s own files, one note for each competency, each industry sector and each piece of evidence, linked to one another. A Claude skill called competency-graph builds that graph with the owner, checks it, counts the evidence and builds this page from it.

Instructions says how to use the page. This tab says what the page and the skill are and what they do. Jump to a topic:

There are three parts, and only one of them is the record. Information moves between them in a loop, and every step that changes the record goes through Claude and is approved by the owner.

The graph is made of three kinds of note and a handful of named links, with two more kinds of note an owner can add. The example below is the smallest graph that uses the three.

Two axes, kept apart

The tree is organised by discipline: what the owner does. The sector, where they did it, is a separate axis reached through the evidence. Putting both in one tree would give nearly every competency two parents and hide the competencies that carry from one industry to another. Methods, tools and standards sit in their own branches under each domain for the same reason.

Each link is stored once

A link is written on the more specific side: a child names its parent, and evidence names what it demonstrates. The reverse direction (a competency’s children, or the evidence behind it) is worked out by reading the notes and is never stored, so the two directions cannot disagree.

Taxonomy, ontology or competency graph

A taxonomy is a tree of categories. An ontology adds typed relationships and rules. A competency graph is connected data: here, competencies with the evidence linked to them. This system is a competency graph built on a taxonomy, with a light ontology on top: two named relations between competencies, notes for organisations and people, and a short list of rules that read them.

The rules that read the relations

Each rule is worked out afresh on every run. It reports or counts, and never writes to a note.

RuleWhat it readsWhat follows
PrerequisiteWhat a competency requires, and the level claimed for each.A claim above a prerequisite’s is reported. No claim is changed, and the supported level is not touched.
One organisationThe organisation on each piece of evidence, and the names an organisation note answers to.Two spellings of one employer count as one organisation, including where a level needs work at two.
Referee as anchorThe people named as referees on evidence.Evidence with a referee counts as having something a third party could check.
Served by, certified byThe links from tools to practices and from credentials to competencies, read backwards.Views only: what serves a practice, and which credentials stand behind a competency. Nothing is counted from them.

How records are stored

Every note is a plain markdown file. Its fields sit in a block at the top of the file and its body is ordinary prose. The record needs no database and no plug-in: the files open in any text editor, and in Obsidian the links appear as backlinks and in the graph view. The only database in the system is the dashboard’s own, which holds changes made on this page until they are applied; it is set out under How this page is built.

A link is the linked note’s filename in double square brackets. Filenames are unique across the folder, so a note can be moved to another subfolder without breaking a link.

How a note is changed

  • A new note is written only after the owner has approved where it goes.
  • Links and fixed-value fields in an existing note are changed only by the skill’s link editor. It runs dry first, checks that every target exists and is the right kind of note, refuses the whole batch if one edit fails, and reads the file again afterwards to prove nothing else changed.
  • A check never edits a note. It reports, and the owner decides what to do about a warning.
  • Where the folder is a git repository, each approved step is a commit, so every change can be reviewed and reversed.

Where the evidence comes from

Nothing in the graph is typed from memory. It is drawn from records the owner already has, in six steps, and each claim can be traced back to one of those records.

  1. The ownerNames the sourcesCVs, job write-ups, credential lists, repositories, publications. Each is marked: becomes evidence, read only, or ignored.
  2. ClaudeCollects candidatesReads the sources in full and lists candidate competencies, each with the file and section it came from.
  3. The ownerDecides the domainsThe candidates are grouped into a few domains named by discipline. Nothing is placed until these are agreed.
  4. The ownerApproves placementsOne domain at a time, Claude proposes each competency’s parent and kind and the evidence links, as a table to approve.
  5. ClaudeWrites the notesCompetency and sector notes are created. An existing job file gains fields; its own text is not touched.
  6. A scriptChecks the resultThe checker must find no errors, and the graph must answer one real question of the owner’s before the next domain.

The record behind a job

An evidence note for a job is the owner’s own write-up of that job with a few fields added, so the graph is only as strong as the write-up. The skill has a method for keeping one, which runs the same way each time the record is brought up to date.

  1. ClaudeGathers, reading onlyThe calendar, mail sent, documents written and tickets closed since the last update.
  2. ClaudeBuilds a ledgerEach candidate achievement with its source, its number and baseline, and how sure it is.
  3. The ownerFills the gapsOne batch of questions: missing numbers, their own part in shared work, what they led.
  4. ClaudeWrites and checksLines to the HAM-Z standard, each read again against its source as a sceptic would.
  5. ClaudeClears the gateBefore a record leaves an employer’s systems, anything confidential is made general or dropped.
  6. The ownerApproves the linksThe competencies shown, those led and any recognition go into the graph.

Each line of a record is written to hold four parts, and each part has somewhere to go in the graph. A line with all four is a HAM-Z line; one without a number from a source is kept as a supporting point, since no number is ever estimated.

Who may see a piece of evidence

Every evidence note carries one of three levels of disclosure, and each output is built at the level that suits its reader.

DisclosureWhere it may appear

A dashboard is built from public evidence, or from public and on-request evidence, and the builder refuses to make one that includes confidential evidence. That evidence is left out of the data files themselves, not only hidden on the page.

From evidence to a supported level

A supported level is never typed in. A script reads every note and works it out, the same way each time.

  1. Collapse into units. Evidence that is part of larger evidence counts with it, so each job, engagement or credential is one independent unit however many projects sit inside it.
  2. Roll up. A unit that shows a narrow competency also supports every broader competency above it, up to the domain.
  3. Count. For each competency: the units and months of delivered work, the units led and the organisations they were led at (one organisation, however the evidence spells it, where an organisation note says so), the years led, the kinds of outside recognition, and the date of the latest work.
  4. Apply the level rule. The highest level whose requirements the counts meet is the supported level. It is then set beside the claimed level, and beside the assessed level where an assessor has rated.

General definitions gives what each level needs and how each count is measured. Two guards are built into the rule: a broader competency does not reach the top two levels just because many narrower ones were each led once, and old evidence is reported as stale but never lowers a level.

Claimed, assessed and supported

A competency can carry three levels, each from a different source. None of them overwrites another, and the differences between them are what the page is for.

ReadingWhere it is keptHow it is arrived at

Each assessor’s ratingIn the same note, as a list with one entry for each assessor: their name, the level and the day.The assessor picks their name on this page, or fills in a worksheet of their own. A new rating replaces only that assessor’s last.

Who the assessors are

The people who can rate are a short list in the settings file, each under an id that never changes, with a name and an optional role. The list is edited on Configuration and waits in the dashboard’s database, like any change made here, until Claude writes it into the settings file. A note records a rating under the assessor’s name, so the note still reads on its own; when a name is changed, Claude moves the ratings recorded under the old one.

When assessors differ

Two assessors who rate a competency at different levels differ by the number of levels between them. That is worked out wherever the ratings are read: the checker reports it, the page marks it beside the assessed level (1) and lists it on Rating gaps, and a job spec is scored on the lower rating. Nothing is changed to make the ratings agree. A difference shows where the people who have seen the work do not yet see it the same way, which is the point of asking more than one.

Frameworks and job specs

A framework is a published list of competencies: a professional body’s, or one the owner wrote. The graph is never reshaped to fit one. A personal competency names each framework competency it answers to, and the framework is read through those names.
